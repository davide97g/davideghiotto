## Code was always a tool

This is the part I think many people get wrong, and got wrong before AI too. Code was
never the goal: it's the means. The real skill is functional reasoning and clarity of
thought — the best developers I've seen were never the ones writing the purest
function.

Now that skill is **the only scarce thing left**. The rest — syntax, boilerplate, the
API call you can't remember — got cheap.

> These tools took my capability **from 0 to 1, not from 10 to 12**.
> — [@ 01:15:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UwMhqq9Evxk&t=4547s)

Things previously infeasible for me became possible: a 3D browser game, this channel,
[sharp](https://sharp.davideghiotto.it/). I didn't get 20% better at the work I already
did: I opened categories of work that were closed.

## The seven levels, and knowing where to stop

I have my own reading — not a standard — of where you sit on the AI spectrum
([@ 00:02:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SpjoxMaH7kw&t=131s)):

1. **Passive consumer** — ChatGPT/Gemini/Claude as an enhanced browser
2. **Prompt power user** — roles, rules, examples; light automation
3. **Vibe coder** — simple apps and sites with [Lovable](https://lovable.dev/), [Bolt](https://bolt.new/), [v0](https://v0.dev/), [Replit](https://replit.com/)
4. **AI user / advanced dev** — you drive the agents: [Cursor](https://cursor.com/), [Claude Code](https://claude.com/claude-code), [Codex](https://openai.com/codex/)
5. **API integrator** — AI becomes a feature of your product
6. **Applied AI engineer** — complex systems: multi-agent orchestration, RAG, tool use, memory
7. **AI researcher** — you create new models. An ontological jump: the researchers inside the frontier labs

The useful part isn't the ranking, it's the **pair of levels**: where you are and where
you want to get to. Knowing where to **stop** is worth as much as knowing where to
climb — professional use and hobby have different targets. Level 7 is
[out of scope](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SpjoxMaH7kw&t=269s) for the channel, and
for 99% of us. Past level 4 the path bends toward the programmer and the applied AI
engineer ([@ 00:03:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SpjoxMaH7kw&t=204s)).

The opposite extreme is the **vibe coder**: someone who builds sites and apps without
being able to program
([@ 00:00:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NTtPD1Olpwo&t=45s)). I don't say that with
contempt — it's proof of how far the barrier to entry dropped.

## On jobs: I'm not pessimistic

> With AI the total amount of work **increases**, and it's higher quality. It doesn't
> mean fewer jobs. — [@ 01:26:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LIvW0-c-kUI&t=5183s)

The friction I actually see isn't economic, it's **human**: some colleagues resist
adoption precisely because these tools automate the manual work they're good at. It's
an enterprise-adoption dynamic, not a layoff wave — and in Italy the rapid US-style
cuts are rare
([@ 01:26:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LIvW0-c-kUI&t=5186s)).

## What I'd tell myself in 2024

**Don't fossilize, and don't throw away what you studied.**

> University and what you studied still count. But don't fossilize: accept the jump.
> — [@ 02:19:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UwMhqq9Evxk&t=8344s)

The two fears are symmetrical and both wrong: "none of what you know matters anymore"
and "it's all hype, I'll carry on as before". What's left is taste, the ability to
steer, and judgment about what's worth building.

Nobody automates those three — for now.
